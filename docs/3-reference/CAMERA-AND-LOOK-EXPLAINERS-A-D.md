# Camera and look explainers, groups A to D

> **[Amnesiac](../4-explanation/concepts/SELF-EXPLAINING-PART.md) landing.** You are planning a short animated film and a field asks for a lens, a camera height, a shot size or a camera move. You do not need to know any camera words. Find the term below. Each one has: what it means in plain words, what it does to the picture, when to pick it, what to type if you are not sure, what a tiny sketch of it would show, two mistakes beginners make, and the source we checked it against.

## How to use this page

1. Find the field's name in the list just below. Four groups: **[A]** how the film looks, **[B]** the lens, **[C]** where the camera stands and what is in the frame, **[D]** how the camera moves.
2. Read **Not sure?** first. It is the safe value. Type it and move on; come back when the shot needs more.
3. Lens numbers here are "35 mm-equivalent" and the camera is "perspective"; both are explained in group [B] (entries *35 mm-equivalent focal length* and *Projection*). If you never change them, nothing goes wrong.
4. The Source line names the book or page we checked the entry against; the table at the end says what each one is. The raw research run that produced these words is `research/corpus/explainers-a-d-plain-language-answers.md`.

Time and motion, editing, scale, light, colour, captions and delivery are separate explainer pages and are not on this one.

## [A] Look development

### Style frame
- **Plain words** One finished test picture that shows exactly how the film should look before you make the rest.
- **On the picture** Nothing yet. It is the target the real frames must match: colors, line, light, mood.
- **Pick it when** Before you draw scene one, like choosing a paint sample before painting the whole room.
- **Not sure?** Make one style frame of your main character in your main place. One is enough to start.
- **Sketch** A small framed picture labeled "target", next to three blank frames with arrows pointing back to it.
- **Mistakes** Making ten frames in ten looks. Skipping it and fixing the look later, one frame at a time.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (pre-production, visual development).

### Mood board
- **Plain words** A page of pictures, colors and textures that together show the feeling you want.
- **On the picture** Not a frame itself. It steers the colors and mood of every frame you make later.
- **Pick it when** At the start, when you can feel the film but cannot describe it yet. Like a collage for a room makeover.
- **Not sure?** Collect six to twelve images you love, then write one line on why each one is there.
- **Sketch** A board with nine small squares: photos, color swatches, a texture, one word like "cozy".
- **Mistakes** Fifty images with no theme. Copying one image on the board instead of using the feeling.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (visual development).

### Reference board
- **Plain words** A board of other people's pictures that show one exact thing, such as how rain or a kitchen looks.
- **On the picture** It tells the artist or image tool what to get right. It never gets copied.
- **Pick it when** You need a detail right, like how a bike's gears look, and a quick look saves guessing.
- **Not sure?** Make one board per thing, and label each image with what it is there to show.
- **Sketch** Four photos of bikes, each with a label: "gears", "handlebars", "wheel size", "seat".
- **Mistakes** No labels, so no one knows what to take from each picture. Shipping a reference image in the film.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (visual development, rights and clearances).

### Look bible
- **Plain words** The one document that collects every rule about how the film looks: colors, lines, light, characters, places.
- **On the picture** Every frame gets checked against it, so frame one and frame two hundred match.
- **Pick it when** More than one person or one tool makes pictures for the film, like a recipe book for a team.
- **Not sure?** Start with one page: style frame, color list, "always" list, "never" list. Grow it as you go.
- **Sketch** A binder cover titled "Look", with tabs: Characters, Places, Colors, Light, Never.
- **Mistakes** Writing it once and never updating it. Keeping rules in chat messages instead of the bible.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (style guide, pre-production).

### Turnaround
- **Plain words** A set of drawings of one character from the front, side, back and three-quarter view, all the same size.
- **On the picture** The character keeps the same shape and height whichever way they face.
- **Pick it when** A character will be seen from more than one side, which is almost always.
- **Not sure?** Four views: front, three-quarter, side, back. Line up the eyes and feet across all four.
- **Sketch** Four small figures in a row, same height, with faint lines through head, waist and feet.
- **Mistakes** Different heights in each view. Only a front view, so the back is guessed every time.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (model sheets); this repo's prop and wardrobe reference (four-view turnaround).

### Color key
- **Plain words** One small painting per scene that fixes the colors and light for that scene.
- **On the picture** Every frame in that scene shares the same palette and time of day.
- **Pick it when** A scene changes mood or time, like a sunny morning that turns into a storm.
- **Not sure?** One color key per scene, painted small and loose. Pick five main colors.
- **Sketch** A strip of five thumbnails, one per scene, each a different blob of colors with the scene number under it.
- **Mistakes** Painting detail instead of color. Letting two frames in one scene use different sunsets.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (color keys, color script).

### Style lock
- **Plain words** One sentence that names the look and tells the image tool what to keep every time.
- **On the picture** The same line, paint and finish in every frame, because the same sentence goes into every prompt.
- **Pick it when** Always. It already exists in this tool as a field on each picture type (the tool calls these render surfaces).
- **Not sure?** Write one sentence: medium, line, color, light. Example: "flat ink lines, soft pastel fills, warm daylight".
- **Sketch** A sticky note with one sentence on it, stuck on the corner of three frames.
- **Mistakes** A paragraph instead of a sentence. Changing it halfway through the film.
- **Source** This repo's continuity card and shot sheet fields; Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (style guide).

### Reference-only provenance
- **Plain words** A tag that says where a picture came from and that it is only for looking at, never for copying or showing.
- **On the picture** Nothing should show it. The tool's checker (the gate) flags a prompt, the text sent to the image tool, naming the reference work.
- **Pick it when** You saved a frame from a show or an artist's page to study. Like a library book you must give back.
- **Not sure?** Tag every outside picture "reference only". Write the source link next to it.
- **Sketch** A photo with a red "REF ONLY" stamp and a line underneath: "from: (link)".
- **Mistakes** Naming the show in your prompt. Letting a reference image slip into the final film.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (rights, clearances and legal review).

### License note
- **Plain words** A short line saying who owns a picture or font and what you are allowed to do with it.
- **On the picture** Nothing. It decides whether the picture may appear in your film at all.
- **Pick it when** Every time you bring in something you did not make, like music, a photo, or a font.
- **Not sure?** Write "license unknown: reference only, do not ship". Find the answer before it ships.
- **Sketch** A card with four blanks: Owner, License, Allowed, Link.
- **Mistakes** Treating "found on the internet" as free. Forgetting fonts and sound.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (rights and clearances).

### Approval status
- **Plain words** A word on each picture that says whether it is still a try, is approved, or was turned down.
- **On the picture** Only approved pictures get copied into the film. Tries and rejects stay out.
- **Pick it when** Someone has to say yes before art moves on, like a teacher checking a draft.
- **Not sure?** Three words: draft, approved, rejected. Everything starts as draft.
- **Sketch** Three frames with a label under each: "draft", "approved" with a green check, "rejected" with a red cross.
- **Mistakes** Using an unapproved frame because it was handy. Deleting rejected tries instead of keeping them labeled.
- **Source** Winder and Dowlatabadi 2019 (approval process, pre-production).

## [B] Optics / lens

### Focal length (mm)
- **Plain words** A number in millimeters that says how zoomed in your camera is. Small number sees wide; big number sees far and close.
- **On the picture** Low numbers fit more in and stretch space. High numbers fit less in and flatten space.
- **Pick it when** A wide room scene wants 24 to 35. A face wants 85. Everyday life looks like 50.
- **Not sure?** Use 50. It is closest to how your own eyes see, so nothing looks strange.
- **Sketch** Three frames of the same person: 24 (whole room, big nose), 50 (normal), 85 (face fills frame, flat).
- **Mistakes** Using a wide lens up close, which bloats faces. Changing the number between two shots of the same moment.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Focal Length; Brown 2016 (lens chapter).

### 35 mm-equivalent focal length
- **Plain words** The lens number translated so every camera uses the same scale, the one old film cameras used.
- **On the picture** Nothing by itself. It makes "50" mean the same view on a phone, a camera, or a software camera.
- **Pick it when** You are writing lens numbers that another tool or person must read the same way.
- **Not sure?** Always write the equivalent number. In this tool the lens field is already the equivalent.
- **Sketch** Two cameras, one big and one small, both pointing at one frame labeled "same view = 50 mm eq."
- **Mistakes** Copying a phone's real lens number (like 6 mm) into the field. Mixing real and equivalent numbers.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Focal Length (separate FX and DX ranges); Brown 2016 (sensor size and lens).

### Angle of view / field of view
- **Plain words** How wide a slice of the world the camera sees, measured in degrees like a slice of pie.
- **On the picture** A big angle shows a lot but small. A small angle shows little but big.
- **Pick it when** Some tools ask for degrees instead of millimeters. 50 mm is about 40 degrees across.
- **Not sure?** Leave it to the lens number. If a tool wants degrees, use about 40.
- **Sketch** A camera seen from above with two lines making a pie wedge; wide wedge labeled 24 mm, narrow wedge 85 mm.
- **Mistakes** Setting both a lens number and an angle that disagree. Using 90 degrees or more, which warps edges.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Focal Length; Blender manual (lens in mm or as field of view).

### Crop factor
- **Plain words** A multiplier that fixes the lens number when a camera's sensor, the chip that catches the picture, is smaller than old film.
- **On the picture** A smaller sensor sees a tighter view from the same lens, as if zoomed in.
- **Pick it when** You copy a lens number from a real small camera and need the equivalent. Phones and small cameras have one.
- **Not sure?** Use 1.0, which means full frame (the old film size) and no fixing needed. Write equivalent numbers and forget it.
- **Sketch** A big rectangle (full frame) with a smaller rectangle inside it (cropped), both around the same picture.
- **Mistakes** Applying it twice. Applying it to a number that was already equivalent.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Focal Length (separate FX and DX ranges); Brown 2016.

### Aperture (f-stop)
- **Plain words** How big the hole in the lens is, written as f and a number. Small number, big hole; big number, small hole.
- **On the picture** A small number (f/2) blurs the background a lot. A big number (f/11) keeps everything sharp.
- **Pick it when** One face must pop from a busy street: f/2. A whole landscape must stay sharp: f/11.
- **Not sure?** Use f/5.6. It gives a little softness behind the subject without hiding the place.
- **Sketch** Two circles, one wide open labeled f/2, one a small dot labeled f/16, each above a frame with blurry or sharp trees.
- **Mistakes** Thinking a bigger number means more blur. Blurring so hard the room disappears.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Maximum Aperture; Blender manual (F-Stop: lower values give more blur).

### T-stop
- **Plain words** Like the f-stop, but measured by how much light really gets through the glass. Used on movie lenses.
- **On the picture** Brightness matches from lens to lens. Blur is the same as the f-stop would give.
- **Pick it when** You are matching real movie footage shot with real lenses. Software cameras do not need it.
- **Not sure?** Ignore it and use the f-stop. In this tool, only the f-stop matters.
- **Sketch** A lens ring marked "T2.8" next to one marked "f/2.8", with an equals sign and "same blur".
- **Mistakes** Treating T and f numbers as two different settings. Filling both with different values.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (lens chapter, f-stops and T-stops).

### Focus distance
- **Plain words** How far from the camera the sharpest point is, in the scene's own units like meters or feet.
- **On the picture** Whatever sits at that distance is crisp. Things nearer or farther go soft.
- **Pick it when** Always. Put it on the thing that matters, usually the eyes of the character who is talking.
- **Not sure?** Set it to the main character's eyes. Many tools let you pick an object instead of a number.
- **Sketch** Camera on the left, a dotted line to a face on the right, a label "focus: 3 m" on the line.
- **Mistakes** Focus on the wall behind the character. Forgetting to move it when the character walks closer.
- **Source** Blender manual (focus object, focus distance); Brown 2016 (lens chapter).

### Focal plane / plane of focus
- **Plain words** An invisible flat wall at the focus distance. Everything touching that wall is sharpest.
- **On the picture** A thin sharp slice of the scene, square to the camera, with softness in front and behind.
- **Pick it when** Two characters must both be sharp: put them the same distance from the camera, on the same wall.
- **Not sure?** Let it sit on the main character's face. You rarely set it apart from focus distance.
- **Sketch** Camera seen from above, a straight line in front of it, two figures standing exactly on the line.
- **Mistakes** One character a step closer, so one face is soft. Expecting a tilted or curved wall; it is flat unless the lens tilts.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (lens chapter); Canon, tilt and shift on TS-E lenses (the plane tilts only with tilt).

### Depth of field
- **Plain words** How much of the scene, from near to far, looks sharp at once.
- **On the picture** Shallow: one sharp face, soft everything else. Deep: sharp from the flowers to the mountains.
- **Pick it when** Shallow for feelings and faces. Deep for places, crowds and action you must follow.
- **Not sure?** Medium. Use f/5.6 and a 50 lens; the character is sharp and the room still reads.
- **Sketch** A row of fence posts; in one frame only the middle post is sharp, in another all posts are sharp.
- **Mistakes** Shallow focus on a group, so half the faces are soft. Blur that hides where the scene happens.
- **Source** Nikon, Understanding Maximum Aperture; Brown 2016 (depth of field).

### Circle of confusion
- **Plain words** How big a point of light can smear before your eye calls it blurry. It sets where "sharp" ends.
- **On the picture** A bigger allowed smear counts more of the scene as sharp; a smaller one counts less.
- **Pick it when** Almost never by hand. Tools use it inside their depth of field math.
- **Not sure?** Leave the tool's default. For full frame it is about 0.03 mm.
- **Sketch** A dot that grows into a soft disc in three steps, with a line marking "still looks sharp" after step two.
- **Mistakes** Changing it to get more blur; use the f-stop instead. Thinking it is a visible circle in the shot.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (depth of field, circle of confusion).

### Hyperfocal distance
- **Plain words** The focus distance where everything from about half that distance to the far horizon stays sharp.
- **On the picture** Nothing soft from the middle ground to the far distance. Good for big places.
- **Pick it when** A landscape or city shot with no single character, like a view from a hill.
- **Not sure?** Do not use it. Focus on the character; let the tool handle the rest.
- **Sketch** Camera at left, a bracket from a midpoint to a far mountain labeled "all sharp".
- **Mistakes** Using it for a face scene, so the face is as sharp as the trash can behind it. Mixing it up with "far focus".
- **Source** Brown 2016 (depth of field, hyperfocal distance).

### Bokeh
- **Plain words** The look of the blurry parts of the picture, especially how out-of-focus lights turn into soft discs.
- **On the picture** Street lights behind a face become round glowing circles. Blur looks creamy or busy.
- **Pick it when** Night scenes with lights behind the character, like a city street or fairy lights.
- **Not sure?** Round and soft. Most tools do this by default when you add blur.
- **Sketch** A dark frame with a sharp face outline and a cluster of soft glowing circles behind it.
- **Mistakes** Asking for bokeh in a bright, sharp scene; there is nothing to blur. Hexagon shapes when the look is soft.
- **Source** Blender manual (aperture blades shape the bokeh); Brown 2016 (lens chapter).

### Tilt-shift
- **Plain words** A lens that can tip or slide sideways instead of staying square to the camera.
- **On the picture** Tilt moves the sharp slice so it leans, or shrinks to a thin band. Shift straightens tall buildings.
- **Pick it when** Tall buildings whose sides must stay straight, or a "toy town" look with a thin sharp band.
- **Not sure?** Off. Keep the lens square. Use the miniature controls below if you want the toy look.
- **Sketch** A camera with its lens pushed up (shift) in one drawing and tipped forward (tilt) in another.
- **Mistakes** Using tilt and expecting straight buildings; that is shift. Overdoing it so the picture looks broken.
- **Source** Canon, The Principles of Tilt and Shift on TS-E lenses.

### Scheimpflug principle
- **Plain words** The geometry rule behind tilt: when the lens tips, the sharp slice tips too, and the three planes meet in one line.
- **On the picture** You can make a table top sharp from near edge to far edge, or make only a thin stripe sharp.
- **Pick it when** You need a tilted sharp slice on purpose, like a long table seen from one end.
- **Not sure?** Skip it. It is the "why" behind tilt, not a setting you type in.
- **Sketch** Three lines (lens, sensor, sharp slice) drawn so they all cross at one point.
- **Mistakes** Trying to set it as a number. Using it when a deeper depth of field would do the same job.
- **Source** Canon, tilt and shift on TS-E lenses (tilting the plane of focus); Brown 2016.

### Miniature faking (synthetic tilt-shift)
- **Plain words** A fake tilt-shift blur that makes a real-size scene look like a tiny model, with a sharp band and soft top and bottom.
- **On the picture** Streets look like toys. Only a flat stripe is sharp; above and below melts away.
- **Pick it when** You want a "toy town" or model-railway feeling, like a city seen from a tall roof.
- **Not sure?** Off. If you try it, use it from a high angle; it barely works at eye level.
- **Sketch** A city from above: a crisp middle stripe, blurry roofs at top, blurry street at bottom.
- **Mistakes** Using it at eye level, which just looks like bad focus. Making the sharp band too thin to read faces.
- **Source** Canon, tilt and shift on TS-E lenses (tilt to restrict sharpness); Blender manual (depth of field).

### Focus band position
- **Plain words** Where the sharp stripe sits in the frame, from 0 at the bottom to 1 at the top.
- **On the picture** Whatever lies on that stripe is sharp; the picture softens away from it.
- **Pick it when** Your subject is not in the middle, like a character low in the frame at 0.3.
- **Not sure?** 0.5, the middle. Then move it until it lands on your character.
- **Sketch** A frame with a flat bar; a ruler at the side marked 0 at the bottom, 1 at the top, bar at 0.5.
- **Mistakes** Leaving it in the middle while the character stands at the bottom. Typing pixels instead of a 0 to 1 number.
- **Source** Canon, tilt and shift (the sharp zone follows the tilted plane); this repo's shot sheet fields.

### Focus band height
- **Plain words** How tall the sharp stripe is, as a share of the frame, from 0 to 1.
- **On the picture** A thin band (0.15) is a strong toy look. A tall band (0.6) is gentle and mostly sharp.
- **Pick it when** A thin band for a model-town feel; a tall band when a face must stay fully sharp.
- **Not sure?** About 0.33, one third of the frame. Faces stay readable and the effect still shows.
- **Sketch** Two frames: one with a thin sharp bar, one with a bar a third of the frame tall.
- **Mistakes** A band thinner than the character's head. Setting height but forgetting position.
- **Source** Canon, tilt and shift (tilt restricts sharpness to a narrow part); Blender manual (depth of field).

### Blur falloff
- **Plain words** How fast the picture goes from sharp to blurry as you leave the sharp stripe.
- **On the picture** Soft falloff fades gently. Hard falloff snaps from crisp to mush in a few lines.
- **Pick it when** Gentle for a dreamy look. Hard for a strong toy look or a comic-style focus stripe.
- **Not sure?** Medium, and the same on top and bottom. It reads as a real lens.
- **Sketch** A gray bar that fades to white slowly on one side and in a sharp step on the other, labeled soft and hard.
- **Mistakes** Different top and bottom falloff by accident. Hard falloff across a face.
- **Source** Blender manual (depth of field); Brown 2016 (depth of field is a gradual zone, not an edge).

### Lens distortion: barrel
- **Plain words** Straight lines bow outward, as if the picture were wrapped around a barrel. Common on wide lenses.
- **On the picture** Door frames and horizons bulge. Faces near the edge look stretched.
- **Pick it when** You want a cheap action-camera or fisheye feel, like a skateboard video.
- **Not sure?** None. Keep lines straight, which is also what most image tools do.
- **Sketch** A square grid whose outer lines bulge outward like a barrel.
- **Mistakes** Adding it to a tidy indoor scene by accident. Combining it with a wide lens on a face.
- **Source** Zeiss, Distortion (Hönlinger and Nasse); Brown 2016 (lens chapter).

### Lens distortion: pincushion
- **Plain words** Straight lines bend inward toward the center, like a cushion pulled tight in the middle. Common on long lenses.
- **On the picture** Edges of walls curve in. Usually small, but it ruins straight buildings.
- **Pick it when** Almost never on purpose. Only when matching real footage from a long zoom lens.
- **Not sure?** None. Leave distortion at zero.
- **Sketch** A square grid whose outer lines pinch inward like a pincushion.
- **Mistakes** Confusing it with barrel; the bend goes the other way. Trying to fix it with the lens number.
- **Source** Zeiss, Distortion (Hönlinger and Nasse).

### Projection: perspective vs orthographic / isometric
- **Plain words** Perspective means far things look smaller, like real eyes. Orthographic means size never changes with distance; isometric is one tipped orthographic view.
- **On the picture** Perspective: roads narrow to a point. Orthographic: parallel lines stay parallel, like a map or a game board.
- **Pick it when** Perspective for stories. Orthographic or isometric for maps, diagrams, or a game-like look.
- **Not sure?** Perspective. It is how people see, and every other term here assumes it.
- **Sketch** Two cubes: one with edges converging (perspective), one with edges parallel (orthographic), tipped so three faces show.
- **Mistakes** Mixing orthographic frames into a perspective film; the jump is jarring. Setting a lens number on an orthographic camera, which does nothing.
- **Source** Blender manual, camera types (perspective, orthographic).

## [C] Camera position and framing

### Camera height
- **Plain words** How high off the ground the camera sits, in the scene's units, such as meters.
- **On the picture** At eye height, people look like equals. Lower makes them tall; higher makes them small.
- **Pick it when** Always. A child's story at a child's eye height feels like being that child.
- **Not sure?** The main character's eye height, even if they are small or sitting. A standing adult's is about 1.6 meters.
- **Sketch** A side view: a figure, a camera on a stand at the figure's eye line, a label with the height.
- **Mistakes** Adult eye height for a scene about a dog or a child. Changing height between two shots of one talk.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera angles and height); Mercado 2013.

### Elevation / tilt angle
- **Plain words** How far the camera is tipped up or down, in degrees. Zero is level. Down is a minus number, so 15 down is -15.
- **On the picture** Tip down and you see floor and the tops of heads. Tip up and you see ceiling and chins.
- **Pick it when** A tall tower needs tipping up. A tabletop needs tipping down.
- **Not sure?** 0 degrees, level, unless you chose a high or low angle on purpose. Otherwise raise or lower the camera, do not tip it.
- **Sketch** A camera seen from the side with a curved arrow; "+20" above, "-20" below, "0" in line with the ground.
- **Mistakes** Tipping up at a building so its walls lean inward. Tipping instead of moving the camera up.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera angles); Mercado 2013 (high and low angle shots).

### High angle
- **Plain words** The camera looks down on the subject from above their eye height.
- **On the picture** The subject looks smaller, weaker, or alone. You see more floor.
- **Pick it when** A character feels lost or small, like a kid alone in a big hallway.
- **Not sure?** Use a gentle one, about 15 degrees down. Big angles shout.
- **Sketch** Camera high at left looking down a slanted line onto a small figure.
- **Mistakes** Using it on the hero in their strong moment. Using it on everyone, so nobody looks strong.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (high angle); Brown 2016.

### Low angle
- **Plain words** The camera looks up at the subject from below their eye height.
- **On the picture** The subject looks bigger, stronger, or scary. You see more ceiling or sky.
- **Pick it when** A character takes charge or a monster appears, like a giant stepping over you.
- **Not sure?** Gentle, about 15 degrees up. Save steep angles for the biggest moments.
- **Sketch** Camera low at left looking up a slanted line at a tall figure against the sky.
- **Mistakes** Low angle on a sad, small moment. Shooting up so close that the nose and chin bloat.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (low angle); Brown 2016.

### Bird's-eye view
- **Plain words** The camera is very high and looks almost straight down, like a bird flying over.
- **On the picture** People turn into dots and patterns. You see the layout of the whole place.
- **Pick it when** Showing a maze, a crowd, or how far apart two people are.
- **Not sure?** Do not use it for talking scenes. Use a wide shot at eye height instead.
- **Sketch** A town square from far above, tiny figures as dots, long shadows.
- **Mistakes** Using it so often it stops feeling special. Faces cannot be read, so no dialogue here.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (high angle, extreme long shot); Brown 2016.

### Top-down / overhead
- **Plain words** The camera points straight down, 90 degrees, at a flat surface or a person below.
- **On the picture** A table or a bed becomes a flat map. Bodies look squashed.
- **Pick it when** Hands working on a desk, a recipe, a map, or someone lying on a bed.
- **Not sure?** Only for flat things like desks and maps. Faces look odd from straight above.
- **Sketch** A camera drawn above a table, arrow straight down, the table shown as a flat rectangle with objects.
- **Mistakes** Trying to show a face this way. Forgetting the camera's shadow would fall into the shot.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera angles, overhead); Mercado 2013.

### Azimuth
- **Plain words** Which way around the character the camera stands, in degrees like a compass: 0 is straight in front.
- **On the picture** 0 is a face-on view. 45 shows the face and one cheek. 90 is a profile. 180 is the back.
- **Pick it when** Two people talking: one at 45 to the left, the other at 45 to the right.
- **Not sure?** 45 degrees, the three-quarter view. It shows the face and gives it shape.
- **Sketch** A top-down circle around a figure, marks at 0 (front), 45, 90 (side), 180 (back), camera at 45.
- **Mistakes** Face-on at 0 for every shot, which looks like a passport photo. Jumping past 90 and flipping which way people face.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (frontal, three-quarter and profile angles).

### Dutch angle (roll)
- **Plain words** The camera is rolled sideways so the horizon slants.
- **On the picture** Walls and floors tilt. The world feels off balance or dangerous.
- **Pick it when** Something is wrong: a lie, a dream, a villain, a sudden shock.
- **Not sure?** 0 degrees, level. Tilting by mistake is the most common framing error.
- **Sketch** A frame with the horizon drawn at a 15 degree slant and a leaning lamppost.
- **Mistakes** A small accidental tilt that just looks sloppy. Using it in a calm scene.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (canted shot); Brown 2016.

### Extreme close-up (ECU)
- **Plain words** The frame shows one small part, like just the eyes or a hand on a key.
- **On the picture** That detail fills the screen. Nothing else is visible.
- **Pick it when** One tiny thing matters: a tear, a trembling finger, a phone screen.
- **Not sure?** Hold it short and use it once. Follow it with a wider shot so viewers know where they are.
- **Sketch** A frame holding only a pair of eyes, eyebrows cut off at the top.
- **Mistakes** Living in it, so the audience loses the room. Cutting through the face at the mouth or chin.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (extreme close up); Brown 2016 (shot sizes).

### Close-up (CU)
- **Plain words** The frame holds the face from about the shoulders up.
- **On the picture** Every feeling on the face is readable. The background is just a hint.
- **Pick it when** A character hears bad news or decides something. Feelings time.
- **Not sure?** Use it for reactions, not for action. Cut the frame at mid chest, not at the neck.
- **Sketch** A frame with a head and the top of the shoulders, a little space above the hair.
- **Mistakes** Cutting at the neck, which looks like a floating head. Too much empty space above the head.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (close up); Brown 2016.

### Medium close-up (MCU)
- **Plain words** The frame holds the head and chest, cut around mid chest.
- **On the picture** The face still reads clearly and you see a bit of posture and clothes.
- **Pick it when** Ordinary talking scenes, like two friends across a table.
- **Not sure?** This or the medium shot are the safest for dialogue. Pick this when the face matters more.
- **Sketch** A frame cut just below the armpits, head near the top third.
- **Mistakes** Cutting exactly at the elbows or the chin. Mixing it with a medium shot of the same person mid-sentence.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (medium close up); Brown 2016.

### Medium shot (MS)
- **Plain words** The frame holds a person from about the waist up.
- **On the picture** You see the face and the hands. Gestures and props read.
- **Pick it when** Someone talks while doing something, like cooking or holding a map.
- **Not sure?** This is the default shot size for most scenes. Start here and move in or out.
- **Sketch** A frame cut at the waist, hands visible, head below the top edge.
- **Mistakes** Cutting at the knees or exactly at the waist joint. Using only medium shots, which feels flat.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (medium shot); Brown 2016.

### Medium wide / cowboy shot (MWS)
- **Plain words** The frame holds a person from mid thigh up, named for westerns that needed the gun belt in view.
- **On the picture** Body language shows, and anything on the hips or in the hands is visible.
- **Pick it when** Stance matters: a standoff, a dance step, a tool on a belt.
- **Not sure?** Use a medium shot unless the hips or a belt item must be seen.
- **Sketch** A frame cut at mid thigh, with a belt and holster clearly inside the frame.
- **Mistakes** Cutting right at the knees. Using it for a close emotional beat.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (shot sizes, cowboy shot); Mercado 2013 (medium long shot).

### Wide shot / long shot (WS / LS)
- **Plain words** The whole person is in the frame, head to feet, with some of the place around them.
- **On the picture** You see where they are and how they move. Faces are small.
- **Pick it when** Showing the place, a walk, a dance, or how two people stand apart.
- **Not sure?** Open each scene with one so people know where they are. Then move in.
- **Sketch** A frame with a full standing figure, floor under the feet, a doorway and furniture around.
- **Mistakes** Cutting off the feet. Expecting the audience to read a small face's expression.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (long shot); Brown 2016.

### Extreme wide shot (EWS / ELS)
- **Plain words** The place is the subject; people are tiny or not even there.
- **On the picture** Sky, land or city fill the frame. A figure is a speck that shows scale.
- **Pick it when** The story moves to a new place, or someone is alone in a big world.
- **Not sure?** One per new location, held a few seconds. Skip it inside small rooms.
- **Sketch** A frame of hills and sky with a tiny figure on a road, smaller than a fingernail.
- **Mistakes** Trying to tell a feeling with it; faces are gone. Using it in a tiny room where it cannot exist.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (extreme long shot); Brown 2016.

### Establishing shot
- **Plain words** The first shot of a scene that shows where and when we are.
- **On the picture** The whole place, often wide, with clues to time of day and weather.
- **Pick it when** The scene jumps to a new place or a new time, like "next morning, at school".
- **Not sure?** Start every new location with one wide shot. Two or three seconds is enough.
- **Sketch** A school front at morning, bus pulling up, sun low, frame labeled "Scene 3 start".
- **Mistakes** Skipping it, so viewers do not know they moved. Making it so long the scene stalls.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (establishing shot); Brown 2016.

### Insert
- **Plain words** A short close shot of an object or detail cut into the scene, like a note or a clock.
- **On the picture** The object fills the frame for a moment so everyone sees it clearly.
- **Pick it when** The audience must read or notice a thing, like a text message or a key turning.
- **Not sure?** Keep it under two seconds and match the light of the scene around it.
- **Sketch** A frame of a hand holding a note with big readable words, nothing else.
- **Mistakes** An insert lit differently from the scene. Showing a detail nobody needs.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (shot types, inserts); Mercado 2013.

### Two-shot
- **Plain words** A frame that holds two people at once.
- **On the picture** You see both faces and the space between them. Their distance tells the relationship.
- **Pick it when** Two characters talk or react together, like sharing a secret or an argument.
- **Not sure?** Medium two-shot, both from the waist up, a little room between them.
- **Sketch** Two figures side by side from the waist up, slightly angled toward each other.
- **Mistakes** One person cut in half at the edge. Both facing the camera like a photo instead of each other.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (two shot); Brown 2016.

### Over-the-shoulder (OTS)
- **Plain words** The camera looks past one person's shoulder at the other person's face.
- **On the picture** A blurry shoulder and ear at the edge; the other face clear in the middle.
- **Pick it when** Conversations. Pair it with the mirror shot from the other side.
- **Not sure?** Put the shoulder in the left third for one person and the right third for the other.
- **Sketch** A dark shoulder and head edge at lower left, a lit face at right center.
- **Mistakes** Shoulder covering half the frame. Jumping to the other side of the pair, so they swap places (see the 180-degree rule).
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (over the shoulder); Brown 2016.

### Point of view (POV)
- **Plain words** The camera shows exactly what a character sees, as if we were inside their head.
- **On the picture** No sign of the character. Their hands may appear. The view moves as they would.
- **Pick it when** We should feel what they feel: peeking through a door, reading a letter, falling.
- **Not sure?** Show the character looking first, then the POV, then their reaction. That tells us whose eyes these are.
- **Sketch** Three small frames: a face looking left; what they see; the face reacting.
- **Mistakes** A POV with no shot of who is looking. Camera height that does not match the character's eyes.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (subjective shot); Brown 2016 (POV).

### Headroom
- **Plain words** The empty space between the top of the head and the top of the frame.
- **On the picture** A little space looks natural. Too much makes the person sink; none makes them cramped.
- **Pick it when** Every shot with a person in it. Tighter shots need less; wider shots need more.
- **Not sure?** Eyes about one third down from the top. That gives the right headroom in most sizes.
- **Sketch** Three frames: too much space above the head, none at all, and a correct small gap with a check mark.
- **Mistakes** Centering the head, which leaves a huge gap above it. Chopping off the top of the hair in a medium shot.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (headroom, composition basics); Brown 2016.

### Lead room / nose room
- **Plain words** Extra space in front of a face or a moving thing, on the side it looks or travels toward.
- **On the picture** A person looking right sits a bit left, so they have room to look into.
- **Pick it when** Someone looks or walks sideways, like a cyclist or a character in profile.
- **Not sure?** Put the character on the third away from where they face. Leave the open side for their gaze.
- **Sketch** A profile face on the left third, empty space on the right, arrow from nose into the space.
- **Mistakes** Face jammed against the edge it looks at. Lead room on the wrong side, so they look out of frame.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (lead room, look room); Brown 2016.

### Rule of thirds
- **Plain words** Split the frame into three columns and three rows, and put important things on the lines or crossings.
- **On the picture** Eyes sit on the top line, horizons on the top or bottom line. Nothing is dead center.
- **Pick it when** Any shot you are unsure how to arrange. It is the starter grid.
- **Not sure?** Eyes on the top third line, horizon on a third line. Center only on purpose.
- **Sketch** A frame with two vertical and two horizontal lines, a face at the top-left crossing.
- **Mistakes** Horizon across the exact middle. Treating it as a law and never breaking it for effect.
- **Source** Mercado 2013 (rule of thirds); Brown 2016 (composition).

### Eyeline
- **Plain words** The direction a character's eyes point, which tells us what they are looking at.
- **On the picture** If two people talk, one looks slightly right and the other slightly left, so they seem to see each other.
- **Pick it when** Every cut between two people, or between a person and a thing.
- **Not sure?** Make each person look toward the other's side of the frame, a little off the lens, never into it.
- **Sketch** Two frames side by side: left face looks right, right face looks left, a dotted line joining their eyes.
- **Mistakes** Both looking the same way, so they seem to look at a third thing. Looking straight into the lens by accident.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (eyelines, cinematic continuity); Mercado 2013.

### 180-degree rule / screen direction
- **Plain words** Draw a line between two characters and keep the camera on one side of it. Left stays left and right stays right.
- **On the picture** The two people never swap sides between cuts. Anyone walking keeps going the same way.
- **Pick it when** Any scene with two people, or a journey across the frame.
- **Not sure?** Pick a side at the start of the scene and stay there. Cross only with a visible camera move.
- **Sketch** Top view: two figures, a dashed line through them, a shaded half circle on one side holding all camera positions.
- **Mistakes** One shot from the far side, so the characters flip. A car driving right, then left, in the same trip.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (the 180 degree line, screen direction); Mercado 2013; this repo's laterality reference.

## [D] Camera movement

### Pan
- **Plain words** The camera stays in one spot and turns left or right, like turning your head.
- **On the picture** The view slides sideways. Near things move faster than far things.
- **Pick it when** Following someone walking past, or showing a room from one place.
- **Not sure?** Slow, and finish on something that matters. Start still, end still.
- **Sketch** A camera on a dot with a curved arrow left to right, two frames showing the start and end views.
- **Mistakes** Panning fast, which smears the picture. Panning across nothing with no reason to stop.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera movement); Mercado 2013 (pan shot).

### Tilt
- **Plain words** The camera stays in one spot and tips up or down, like nodding.
- **On the picture** The view climbs a tower or slides from a face down to hands.
- **Pick it when** Revealing something tall, or moving from feet to face as a character enters.
- **Not sure?** Slow, and end level if people will talk next.
- **Sketch** A camera on a dot with a curved arrow up and down, frames showing floor then ceiling.
- **Mistakes** Confusing it with crane up; tilt turns, crane lifts. Ending on a slanted, empty frame.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera movement); Mercado 2013 (tilt shot).

### Dolly push-in
- **Plain words** The whole camera rolls forward toward the subject.
- **On the picture** The subject grows and the background slides past them. It feels like leaning in.
- **Pick it when** A realization, a secret, a moment that tightens, like reading a hidden message.
- **Not sure?** Slow and short: from a medium shot to a close-up over three to five seconds.
- **Sketch** Camera on wheels with an arrow toward a figure, two frames: smaller figure, then larger.
- **Mistakes** Pushing in on nothing happening. Using zoom instead; zoom does not move the background the same way.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (move in, dolly); Mercado 2013 (dolly shot).

### Dolly pull-out
- **Plain words** The whole camera rolls backward, away from the subject.
- **On the picture** The subject shrinks and the place grows around them. It feels like letting go or being left alone.
- **Pick it when** An ending, a loss, or revealing where someone really is.
- **Not sure?** Slow. End on a wide shot that could be the last shot of the scene.
- **Sketch** Camera on wheels with an arrow away from a figure, two frames: large figure, then small in a big room.
- **Mistakes** Pulling out in the middle of a conversation. Backing into a wall in the scene's layout.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (move out, dolly); Mercado 2013 (dolly shot).

### Truck
- **Plain words** The whole camera slides sideways, left or right, keeping its facing.
- **On the picture** Like looking out a train window: near things rush, far things crawl.
- **Pick it when** Walking beside a character, or passing along a row of things.
- **Not sure?** Match the character's walking speed and keep them in the same spot in frame.
- **Sketch** Camera on a rail with an arrow sideways, a figure walking in the same direction beside it.
- **Mistakes** Mixing it up with pan; truck moves, pan turns. Letting the character drift out of frame.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (tracking and crab moves); Mercado 2013 (tracking shot).

### Pedestal
- **Plain words** The whole camera rises or lowers straight up or down, staying level, like an elevator.
- **On the picture** The view rises along a body or a shelf without tipping. Horizons stay level.
- **Pick it when** Moving from a child's eye height to an adult's, or climbing a bookshelf.
- **Not sure?** Use tilt instead unless lines must stay straight. Keep it slow.
- **Sketch** A camera on a pole with an up-down arrow, frames showing shoes then face, both level.
- **Mistakes** Confusing it with tilt. Rising so high the character's head leaves the frame.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (boom up and down, camera movement).

### Crane / jib
- **Plain words** The camera swings on a long arm, so it can fly up, over, and down in one move.
- **On the picture** A sweeping lift from a face to a whole street, or a drop from the sky to a door.
- **Pick it when** Big openings and endings, or revealing how big a place is.
- **Not sure?** Use one at the start or end of the film only. Keep the move smooth and slow.
- **Sketch** A camera on a long arm with a pivot, a curved arrow from low to high, two frames: a face, a rooftop view.
- **Mistakes** Craning in a small talking scene. Fast swings that make the picture swim.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (crane moves); Mercado 2013 (crane shot).

### Arc / orbit
- **Plain words** The camera circles around the subject while keeping it in the middle of the frame.
- **On the picture** The background spins behind a steady subject. It feels like a big moment or a trap.
- **Pick it when** A character stands in the eye of a storm: a decision, a dance, a surrounded hero.
- **Not sure?** A quarter circle, slow. Full circles get dizzy fast.
- **Sketch** Top view: a figure in the center, a camera on a circle around them with an arrow along the circle.
- **Mistakes** Crossing the 180 line without meaning to; it flips left and right. Spinning twice.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (circle track, arc); Mercado 2013.

### Zoom (optical)
- **Plain words** The camera stays still and the lens changes its number, so the view magnifies without the camera moving.
- **On the picture** Everything gets bigger together and space flattens. The background does not slide like a dolly.
- **Pick it when** A quick jab of attention, like a news camera finding a face in a crowd.
- **Not sure?** Use a dolly instead for most shots. If you zoom, keep it short.
- **Sketch** A still camera, a frame with a small face, then the same frame with the face big and the background big too.
- **Mistakes** Zooming in a dreamy scene where a dolly would feel real. Slow zooms that nobody notices but that flatten the picture.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (zoom versus dolly); Mercado 2013 (zoom shot).

### Digital zoom
- **Plain words** Cropping the picture and stretching it bigger, with no lens change.
- **On the picture** The same picture gets grainy and soft. Nothing about the view actually changes.
- **Pick it when** Only when fixing a frame later and you have more pixels than you need.
- **Not sure?** Never in the plan. Change the lens number or move the camera instead.
- **Sketch** A frame, then a small rectangle inside it blown up to full size with jagged edges.
- **Mistakes** Treating it like a real zoom. Zooming past the size your delivery needs.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (punch-in versus optical zoom; image resolution).

### Rack focus / focus pull
- **Plain words** The sharp point moves from one thing to another during the shot, like your eyes refocusing.
- **On the picture** A face goes soft as the phone behind it turns sharp, or the other way around.
- **Pick it when** Two things in one frame matter one after the other, like a face and then what it sees.
- **Not sure?** One rack per shot, slow enough to see. Start sharp on what matters first.
- **Sketch** Two frames: sharp face and blurry phone, then blurry face and sharp phone, with an arrow between.
- **Mistakes** Racking in a deep-focus shot, where nothing changes. Racking so fast it reads as a mistake.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (focus pulls); Blender manual (focus object, focus distance).

### Handheld
- **Plain words** The camera is carried by a person, so it shakes and drifts a little.
- **On the picture** A nervous, real, "I was there" feeling. Lines wobble slightly.
- **Pick it when** Panic, chases, news, or a scene that should feel raw.
- **Not sure?** Off. Use a steady camera. If you want a little life, add a tiny slow drift.
- **Sketch** A frame with slightly wavy edges and a small arrow cluster showing jitter.
- **Mistakes** So much shake that viewers feel sick. Handheld in a calm scene for no reason.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (handheld and steady-rig shots); Mercado 2013.

### Locked-off
- **Plain words** The camera sits on a stand and does not move at all during the shot.
- **On the picture** A perfectly still frame. Only the characters move. It feels calm and clear.
- **Pick it when** Most dialogue scenes, jokes that need a still frame, and any time in doubt.
- **Not sure?** This is the safe default for every shot. Add a move only when a move entry's feeling matches your scene.
- **Sketch** A camera on a three-legged stand with a small padlock icon, a frame with a still room.
- **Mistakes** Thinking a still camera is boring; still frames make movement inside them stronger. Adding a drift on the next shot without meaning to.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (static shots, motivated movement).

### Drone / aerial
- **Plain words** The camera flies high above the ground, moving freely over the scene.
- **On the picture** Rooftops, roads and fields sweep by. People are tiny.
- **Pick it when** Opening a film over a town, or a journey across land.
- **Not sure?** One at the start, slow and straight. Do not fly during conversations.
- **Sketch** A camera with small propellers above a town, an arrow forward, a frame of rooftops.
- **Mistakes** Flying in a scene with faces that must be read. A spinning camera that loses the direction of the journey.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (aerial shots, camera movement).

### Parallax
- **Plain words** When the camera moves, near things slide across the picture faster than far things.
- **On the picture** Depth you can feel: a fence whips past, hills barely move. This is what makes a dolly feel real.
- **Pick it when** You want a flat scene to feel deep: place something near, something mid, something far, then move.
- **Not sure?** Let it happen. Put at least one object close to the camera before any dolly or truck.
- **Sketch** Two frames of a sideways move: the near post jumps a long way, the far tree barely moves, arrows of different length.
- **Mistakes** Moving the camera in a scene with nothing near, so it feels like a zoom. Painting layers that all slide at one speed.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (dolly versus zoom; depth cues in camera movement).

### Move speed
- **Plain words** How fast the camera moves, written as a word like slow, or as units per second.
- **On the picture** Slow feels calm or tense. Fast feels urgent or playful. Too fast smears the picture.
- **Pick it when** Every moving shot. Match the speed to the mood and to the character's own speed.
- **Not sure?** Slow. For a push-in, cover the distance in three to five seconds.
- **Sketch** A frame with three arrows labeled slow, medium, fast, the fast one with a blur trail.
- **Mistakes** Fast moves on a soft lens, which smears. Different speeds for the same kind of move in one scene.
- **Source** Brown 2016 (camera movement, motivated moves); Lasseter 1987 (timing).

### Easing (ease-in / ease-out)
- **Plain words** The move starts slowly, speeds up, then slows to a stop, instead of jerking on and off.
- **On the picture** Smooth, natural motion, like a real hand pushing a cart. No sudden jolts.
- **Pick it when** Every camera move that starts or stops inside the shot.
- **Not sure?** On, at both ends. Most tools call it "ease in and out" or "slow in, slow out".
- **Sketch** A speed curve shaped like a gentle hill next to a boxy curve with sharp corners labeled "no easing".
- **Mistakes** Turning it off, so moves snap. Easing so long the camera never reaches speed.
- **Source** Lasseter 1987 (slow in and slow out); Brown 2016 (camera movement).
